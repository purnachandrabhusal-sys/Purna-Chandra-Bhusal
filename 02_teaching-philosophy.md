**My Teaching Philosophy**


There are four underlying principles that inform my writing pedagogy: (1) writing is a meaningful activity, (2) writing fosters students' agency, (3) writing requires rhetorical awareness, and (4) writing is a wise activity. Grounded in my years of higher education journey, teaching experiences and reflections, these principles inform my design of syllabi, discussions, and assignments. 

Meaningfulness, for me, means finding (or better creating) a justifiable and motivating rationale behind every writing that students do. The principle of meaningfulness in teaching writing encourages me to design various activities that create a driving force that motivates and fully engages students in 'their' writing. As a facilitator, I take it as my responsibility to discuss with students why each act of writing matters before they actually write. To do so, I invite students to think about compelling writing topics that they feel they are connected to; they brainstorm possible topics of writing regardless of the genre and modes they are working in. In my experience, teaching writing is successful when I design peer discussion sessions followed by writing activities that give students opportunities to write or collaborate on topics that truly interest them. I use the principle of meaningfulness to collaborate with students to answer the question of 'why learn writing' or 'why write'. 

Meaningfulness, however, does not exist in isolation but is essentially connected to students' agency. I believe that the principle of ‘fostering students' agency’ in writing refers to designing writing classrooms to promote students’ independent and critical thinking throughout their writing process. I take it as my responsibility to create more choices or topics of writing for each student to honor their unique voices/interests/purpose/criticality in their writing. Before they write, I invite students to discuss topics of interest from multiple perspectives, sometimes through a challenging or completely different lens, and ask them to justify and rationalize their own writing ideas and insights.  I promote agential writing through activities like group discussion, quizzes, peer review, discussion board replies, etc. I use the principle of ‘fostering students’ agency’ to address the question of ‘how to think prior to or while writing’.

Meaningful and agential writing is a rhetorical act for me. I believe that every act of teaching writing or writing necessitates rhetorical awareness in both the instructors and students. I apply the lens of rhetorical awareness by encouraging students to think of the rhetorical situation or context that influences their writing. For that purpose, I introduce the concept of purpose or motive, audience, the medium, genre, tone, modality, etc. Through the lens of rhetorical awareness, I further encourage students to decide the formal and textual aspects of their writing: organization, diction, tone, clarity, (multi)modality, accessibility, size/length, and more. I use the principle of rhetorical awareness to enhance writing insights across all stages of the writing process, from brainstorming to editing. I use the principle of rhetorical awareness to address the question of fully situating writing in context and achieving its purpose.

Writing or teaching writing is a wise act for me. In my understanding, meaningfulness, agency, and rhetorical acts can be fully operationalized by incorporating the principle or lens of wisdom, which is uniquely human. Wisdom, in the context of teaching writing, is an act of doing or being just and virtuous in words, ideas, and designs across writing/composition contexts. Using the lens of wisdom, I aim to accommodate all kinds of learning or writing dis/abilities or communities in my classroom. I use the wisdom lens in the writing classroom, inviting students to rethink what they can contribute to the community and the world by composing various texts, digital or non-digital. Amid the conversation of generative artificial intelligence (Gen AI), I use the principle of wisdom to spark the concept of academic integrity, ethics, honesty, kindness, empathy, impact, community-engaged writing, etc. I use the principle of wisdom to address the questions of ‘ethical and accessible choices in writing’.

In sum, my teaching of writing is guided by the concepts of meaningfulness, students' agency, rhetorical awareness, and wisdom. However, despite the four principles or pedagogical approaches I discussed, I believe that teaching writing is an ever-evolving act that is always in a continuum, in which an instructor’s job is to adapt to emerging pedagogical approaches to fully achieve learning outcomes across diverse classroom, institutional, departmental, curricular, and intellectual settings.
